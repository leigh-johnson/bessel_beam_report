The first generation Strontium optical lattice clock in our lab resolved gravitational redshift measurements across 5 atom ensembles across a 1 cm vertical path \cite{zhengLabbasedTestGravitational2023}. \section{Experimental and Theoretical Approaches}
\subsection{Bessel Beams and Bessel-Gauss Beams}

On the plane transverse to the propagation direction, an ideal Bessel beam (BB) has uniform intensity distribution described by a Bessel function of the first kind, $J_0$. This property led to the description of BBs as non-diffracting beams, since the transverse intensity profile does not change as the beam propagates through space \cite{durninDiffractionfreeBeams1987}. The ideal Bessel beam is non-diffracting over a theoretically infinite range. The transverse intensity distribution of an ideal Bessel beam has the form:
\begin{equation}
  I(r) \propto J_0^2(k_r \cdot r),
  \label{eq:ideal_bessel_beam}
\end{equation}
where $k_r$ is the transverse wavevector satisfying $k_r^2 + k_z^2 = k^2 = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $r$ is the radial distance from the propagation axis.

Bessel beams can be realized by passing a Gaussian beam through an axicon lens (another name for a conical prism), which converts the Gaussian input into a conical wavefront that interferes constructively along the propagation axis to form a Bessel-Gauss beam \cite{raoConceptualReviewBessel2024}. A Gaussian beam propagating through an axicon of base angle $\alpha$ and refractive index $n$ is deflected toward the axis by $\beta = \arcsin(n \sin \alpha) - \alpha$, creating a Bessel-Gauss beam of the zeroth order. The Bessel-Gauss beam has a transverse intensity profile described by \autoref{eq:ideal_bessel_beam}, where $k_r = (2\pi/\lambda)\sin\beta$ is governed by the axicon deflection angle $\beta$ and the wavelength $\lambda$ of the input Gaussian beam.

\subsection{Geometric / Ray Model}
A three-axicon design was inspired by \cite{schniederCollimatingThreeaxiconZoom2024}. The geometric model of a three-axicon Bessel beam system is shown in \autoref{fig:theory_geometric_model}. Axicon~1 converts the Gaussian input beam into a cone of rays deflected by $\beta_1$. Axicon~2 collimates them into an annulus of inner radius $\rho_{\text{in}}$ and outer radius $\rho_{\text{out}}$. Axicon~3 deflects the annulus rays by $\theta$. The region where the rays overlay on axis (red) is the geometric Bessel range. $L_{12}$ and $L_{23}$ are the axicon separations.

\section{Results}
A suitable candidate meeting all design requirements was not found using 1 in. diameter axicon lenses with base angles $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \SI{5}{\degree}$ and $\alpha_3=0.\SI{5}{\degree}$. The measured Bessel zone for each configuration is summarized in \autoref{tab:results_summary} and \autoref{fig:measured_window_envelopes_lobe_diam_colored}, along with the modeled geometric and QDHT ranges. Four candidates were evaluated, with a description of each in \autoref{tab:configs}. The QDHT simulation consistently predicted the starting point of the Bessel range for three candidates within $9\%$ agreement. In two out of four candidates, the Bessel zone length was within $8\%$ agreement with the QDHT simulation. Comparatively, the geometric model consistently predicted the start of the Bessel zone within $3.6\%$ to $5.5\%$ agreement but overestimated the Bessel zone length by $+42\%$ to $+89\%$.

The measured Bessel zone was imaged using a monochrome CMOS camera mounted on a motorized 3-axis CNC gantry, which allowed us to sample the beam profile along \SI{2}{\meter} of propagation with repeatable sub-millimeter positioning. See Experimental Setup for details about the experimental setup and optics design candidates. The core diameter and FWHM were extracted from $J_0^2$ fits within the measured Bessel zone (see \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_jul29}).

Candidate \#1 was the baseline configuration, with a measured Bessel zone length of $\Delta Z_R = 0.38 \pm 0.01$ m, within $\approx$\SI{1}{\centi\meter} agreement with the length predicted by the QDHT model. The measured Bessel zone range $Z_R \in [1.29, 1.68]$ m was $\approx$10 cm from the predicted QDHT range. The mean measured core diameter and FWHM were within 2\% of the ideal $J_0^2$ values at all points measured within the Bessel zone, with a mean core diameter of \SI{126.4}{\micro\meter} and FWHM of \SI{59.2}{\micro\meter}.

Candidate \#2 used a $2\times$ magnification telescope before axicon~1 to expand the Gaussian input beam, resulting in a measured Bessel zone length of $\Delta Z_R > 0.83$ m. Due to space limitations on the optics table, the Bessel zone was not fully characterized. The spacing between axicon~1 and axicon~2 ($L_{12} =$ \SI{240}{\milli\meter}) was the maximum allowed by the 1 in. aperature of axicon~2. The measured Bessel zone range $Z_R \in [1.18, >2.01]$ m was $\approx$ 10 cm from the predicted QDHT range. The mean measured core diameter and FWHM were within 5\% of the ideal $J_0^2$ values at all points measured within the Bessel zone, with a mean core diameter of \SI{118.2}{\micro\meter} and FWHM of \SI{55.4}{\micro\meter}.

\subsection{Ripples in On-Axis Intensity}
The ripple \% metric is defined as the ratio of the peak-to-peak variation in the on-axis intensity $I(0,z)$ to the mean on-axis intensity within the measured Bessel zone. The on-axis intensity was not uniform within the measured Bessel zone for
any candidate (\autoref{fig:measured_window_envelopes_lobe_diam_colored}).
The measured ripple ranged from 48\% to 63\% of the mean on-axis intensity
(\autoref{tab:results_summary}). Uniformity of clock interrogation intensity was stated as a requirement for the Bessel beam design. Although no acceptable threshold has yet been defined for the on-axis intensity uniformity, the measured ripple was significant enough to rule out all candidates.

We initially considered whether the on-axis intensity ripples were a numerical artifact of the simulation's discrete Fast-Fourier Transform (FFT). However, the ripples persisted across a wide range of sampling parameters. Initial measurements with a manual translation stage (see figure TODO) were too coarse to resolve them, but finer scans with the motorized gantry revealed on-axis intensity modulations in agreement with those predicted analytically by Rao~\cite{raoOnaxisIntensityModulationfree2018} and reproduced by the QDHT simulations.

\subsection{Axicon Alignment Drift}
To test repeatability, we re-measured three $z$ positions of candidate
\#2's Bessel zone five days after the original scan
(\autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_jul29} vs. \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_aug3}). The intensity ripples were reproduced and the fitted core diameters agreed within a few percent, but the peak intensity dropped from 0.48 to 0.33 counts/\si{\micro\second}. The drift did not occur uniformly. In \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_aug3}, the X-chord (blue dots) shifted more than the Y-chord (orange dots), resulting in a slight astigmatism. Fixing the astigmatism required systematically adjusting the horizontal axes of all XY translation mounts to locate the source of the astigmatism.

\subsection{Effect of 2x Telescope}
Candidate \#4 placed a $2\times$ magnification telescope inside the
Bessel zone, $z = \SI{120}{\milli\meter}$ after axicon~3
(\autoref{tab:configs}), to understand the effect of magnification on the Bessel beam. The mean core diameter of \SI{268.1}{\micro\meter} is within $7.8\%$ of
twice the ideal core size, confirming the expected $2\times$ transverse
magnification in the XY plane. The peak intensity dropped to 0.32 counts/\si{\micro\second},
compared to 2.00 for the identical optics without the telescope
(candidate \#3), which is roughly consistent with spreading the same power over a $4\times$ larger area ($2\times$ in X and $2\times$ in Y) with significant ($\approx$ 0.7 counts/us) loss at the telescope lenses.

The measured Bessel zone length was $\Delta Z_R = 0.32 \pm 0.01$ m, only slightly longer than the $\Delta Z_R = 0.30 \pm 0.01$ m measured for candidate \#3 without the telescope. This result was not consistent with the expected $2\times$ longitudinal magnification of the Bessel zone. However, gaps in the dataset at $z \approx 0.2$m could explain this discrepancy if the missing data contains a ripple peak that would extend the measured Bessel zone length.

\section{Appendix A: Optics Design Candidates and Experimental Setup}\label{sec:experimental_setup}
Axicon lenses in \autoref{tab:configs} were sourced from Thorlabs (AX255-A, AX2505-A), manufactured from UV Fused Silica substrate ($n \approx 1.4565$ at 650nm) with AR coating for 350-700 nm range.

An adjustable focus laser diode (Thorlabs CPS650F) was coupled to a single-mode fiber using a fiber optic coupler (Schafter+Kirchhoff 60FC-L-4-M25-24) to produce a Gaussian input beam with $1/e^2$ diameter of $D_{\text{in}} = \SI{4.59}{\milli\meter}$ at axicon~1. The laser diode wavelength was nominally given as $\lambda = \SI{650}{\nano\meter}$, with a measured $\SI{1.2}{\milli\watt}$ power output after the fiber coupling.

Axicon lenses were mounted in XY translation mounts for alignment, and the axicon separations $L_{12}$ and $L_{23}$ were measured using a stainless steel ruler. The Bessel zone was imaged using a monochrome CMOS camera (FLIR BFS-PGE-31S4M-C) with 2048x1536 resolution with a \SI{3.45}{\micro\meter} pixel size. The camera was mounted on a translation stage to image the Bessel zone at different $z$ positions.
