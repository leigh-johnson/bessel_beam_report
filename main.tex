\begin{abstract}

  Optical lattice clocks can resolve gravitational redshift at tabletop scale, but extending measurements across a \SI{1}{\meter} vertical baseline requires a clock-interrogation beam with uniform intensity over the full path. We designed, built, and characterized a three-axicon system that forms a zeroth-order Bessel beam from 1~in.\ optics and evaluated four configurations for strontium clock interrogation. Using a purpose-built motorized three-axis imaging system, we profiled the beam over \SI{2}{\meter} of propagation. All four produced Bessel cores with mean diameter within 8\% of the ideal $J_0^2$ value, but none met the required \SI{300}{\micro\meter} FWHM. The widest measured central lobe was \SI{125.6}{\micro\meter}. A quasi-discrete Hankel transform (QDHT) model with no free parameters predicted Bessel-zone start within 10\% and length within 8\% for two of three modeled configurations. A geometric ray model predicted the start comparably well but overestimated zone length by 42--89\%. The longest measured zone was ${>}\SI{0.83}{\meter}$ against a \SI{1}{\meter} target, while on-axis intensity varied by 48--63\% peak-to-peak in all measured zones. The 1~in.\ aperture limits axicon separation $L_{12}$, constraining zone length and influencing intensity ripple. None of the four configurations met all design requirements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Background and Aims}\label{sec:background}
Optical lattice clocks are precise enough to resolve the gravitational
redshift at tabletop scale. The first-generation strontium clock in our
lab resolved the redshift across five atom ensembles spanning a
\SI{1}{\centi\meter} vertical path
\cite{zhengLabbasedTestGravitational2023}. The second-generation clock
extends this to three ensembles across \SI{1}{\meter}. A vertical
optical lattice traps the strontium atoms, and a co-propagating
\SI{698}{\nano\meter} clock laser interrogates (probes) them. Since the
redshift is proportional to height, intensity variations along the
interrogation path introduce a systematic error that must be
characterized. The clock beam would ideally be as uniform as possible across over a meter of propagation, which is not possible with a conventional focused Gaussian beam (see \autoref{fig:theory_gaussian_vs_bessel_intro}). A Bessel
beam is a promising candidate because it has a non-diffracting profile over long propagation distances~\cite{durninDiffractionfreeBeams1987,
  durninComparisonBesselGaussian1988, raoConceptualReviewBessel2024}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{gaussian_vs_bessel_intro}
  \caption{\label{fig:theory_gaussian_vs_bessel_intro} Comparison of an ideal Gaussian beam and zeroth-order Bessel beam. (a)~Transverse intensity profile of a Gaussian beam at its waist. (b)~Transverse intensity profile $I(r) \propto J_0^2(k_r \cdot r)$ of an ideal Bessel beam, showing the central lobe and surrounding concentric rings. (c)~Longitudinal intensity profile of a Gaussian beam, showing how the beam's width grows (diverges) as it propagates. (d)~The Bessel beam's central lobe stays constant/uniform. }
\end{figure}
This study addresses one primary question: can a three-axicon system built from 1~in. optics produce a Bessel zone that meets the following requirements for clock interrogation:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Bessel zone length of at least \SI{1}{\meter}.
  \item Central lobe of the Bessel region is large enough to contain a cloud of strontium atoms (cross-sectional FWHM at least \SI{300}{\micro\meter} in diameter).
  \item Uniform on-axis intensity profile.
  \item Bessel region fits within 1/2~in.\ mirror (mirror 1, entrance to science chamber).
  \item Annulus after Bessel region is larger than 1/2~in.\ (minimize reflections off mirror 2 in the science chamber).
\end{enumerate}
We tested this by building four candidate configurations (\autoref{tab:configs}) and comparing the measured Bessel zone of each against models described in \autoref{sec:geometric_model} and \autoref{sec:qdht_model}.

\begin{figure*}[!t]

Axicon~2 ($\alpha_2=\SI{5}{\degree}$) collimates them into an annulus of inner radius $\rho_{\text{in}}$ and outer radius $\rho_{\text{out}}$. \section{Experimental and Theoretical Approaches}

\subsection{Bessel Beams and Bessel-Gauss Beams}

On the plane transverse to the propagation direction, an ideal Bessel beam (BB) has an invariant intensity distribution described by a Bessel function of the first kind, $J_0$. This property led to the description of BBs as non-diffracting beams, since the transverse intensity profile does not change as the beam propagates through space~\cite{durninDiffractionfreeBeams1987}. The ideal Bessel beam is non-diffracting over a theoretically infinite range. The transverse intensity distribution of an ideal Bessel beam has the form:
\begin{equation}
  I(r) \propto J_0^2(k_r \cdot r),
  \label{eq:ideal_bessel_beam}
\end{equation}
where $k_r$ is the transverse wavevector satisfying $k_r^2 + k_z^2 = k^2 = (2\pi/\lambda)^2$ and $r$ is the radial distance from the propagation axis.

Passing a Gaussian beam through an axicon (another name for a conical prism) produces a Bessel-Gauss beam by converting the Gaussian input into a conical wavefront that interferes constructively along the propagation axis \cite{raoConceptualReviewBessel2024}. An axicon of base angle $\alpha$ and refractive index $n$ deflects the Gaussian beam toward the axis by $\beta = \arcsin(n \sin \alpha) - \alpha$, creating a Bessel-Gauss beam of the zeroth order. The Bessel-Gauss beam has a transverse intensity profile described by \autoref{eq:ideal_bessel_beam}, where $k_r = (2\pi/\lambda)\sin\beta$ is governed by the axicon deflection angle $\beta$ and the wavelength $\lambda$ of the input Gaussian beam.

\subsection{Geometric / Ray Model}\label{sec:geometric_model}
We based the design on the collimating three-axicon zoom system described in \citet{schniederCollimatingThreeaxiconZoom2024}. \autoref{fig:theory_geometric_model} shows the geometric model of our system and defines the ray angles and axicon separations. Because the annulus leaving axicon~1 keeps expanding as it propagates, the 1~in.\ aperture of axicon~2 sets an upper limit on $L_{12}$. Within that limit, increasing $L_{12}$ suppresses the on-axis intensity ripple (\autoref{fig:theory_three_axicon_L12}).

\subsection{Wave Optics / QDHT Model}\label{sec:qdht_model}
The geometric model predicts where rays overlap, but not the intensity within the Bessel zone. To get a sense of the idealized intensity distribution, we constructed a wave-optics simulation. We first attempted the analysis in Zemax software, whose physical optics propagation uses a fast Fourier transform (FFT) on a square Cartesian grid~\cite{ZemaxOpticStudio}. We discarded this approach out of concern that design decisions would be governed by non-physical artifacts of the square sampling grid.

Since the idealized optical system is rotationally symmetric about the propagation axis, we were able to model the field propagation with a quasi-discrete Hankel transform (also known as a Fourier-Bessel transform) described by \citet{yuQuasidiscreteHankelTransform1998a}.

Our QDHT model implementation accepts only measured quantities as inputs: the Gaussian input beam waist $w_0$, the axicon base angles $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$, and $\alpha_3$, the axicon separations $L_{12}$ and $L_{23}$, and the wavelength $\lambda$. The model has no free parameters fit to our data. The QDHT models in \autoref{fig:measured_window_envelopes_lobe_diam_colored} are sampled at $N=4096$ points in the radial direction over a domain padded to $2.5\times$ the Gaussian input beam radius.


We implemented both geometric and QDHT models in an open-source Python simulator with an interactive web interface\footnotemark[1]. Parameter sweeps over the axicon angles, separations, and input beam diameter are collected in \autoref{sec:model_parameter_sweeps}.
\footnotetext[1]{\url{https://bessel-beam.streamlit.app/}}

We coupled an adjustable-focus laser diode (Thorlabs CPS650F, nominal
$\lambda = \SI{650}{\nano\meter}$) to a single-mode fiber with a fiber
optic coupler (Schafter+Kirchhoff 60FC-L-4-M25-24), producing a Gaussian
input beam with $1/e^2$ diameter $D_{\text{in}} = \SI{4.59}{\milli\meter}$
at axicon~1 and \SI{1.2}{\milli\watt} of power after the coupler.

We mounted three UV fused silica axicons (Thorlabs AX255-A and AX2505-A;
$n \approx 1.4565$ at \SI{650}{\nano\meter}, AR-coated for
350--\SI{700}{\nano\meter}; $\alpha_1 = \alpha_2 = \SI{5}{\degree}$,
$\alpha_3 = \SI{0.5}{\degree}$) in XY translation mounts and measured the
separations $L_{12}$ and $L_{23}$ with a stainless steel ruler. We
aligned the system by centering the annulus on each successive optic
(``beam walking''), using the beam profiler gantry as the alignment
tool. The four candidate configurations we evaluated are listed in
\autoref{tab:configs}.

We characterized the Bessel zone by imaging the beam through an OD=4.0
filter directly onto a monochrome CMOS sensor (FLIR BFS-PGE-31S4M-C,
$2048 \times 1536$ pixels, \SI{3.45}{\micro\meter} pixel size) mounted
on a motorized three-axis CNC gantry built for this project, which allowed us to sample the beam profile along \SI{2}{\meter} of propagation with repeatable sub-millimeter positioning. The gantry has \SI{130}{\milli\meter} of travel along the propagation direction and \SI{100}{\milli\meter} of travel in the transverse XY plane. Because the $z$ travel is shorter than the region of interest, we repositioned the gantry by hand along the optical table to cover the full \SI{2}{\meter} propagation path. Each placement is registered against a sheet of \SI{1}{\centi\meter} graph paper fixed to the table (\autoref{fig:gantry_isometric_photo}), so the measurement position is repeatable between runs. At each plane the acquisition software locates the beam and sets the exposure time automatically, so that the brightest pixel in the plane falls below a saturation threshold. It also collects dark frames for background subtraction. If the beam diameter is larger than the camera sensor, the profiler software collects a grid of images and stitches them into a single composite image.

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:metrics}
From each plane, we crop the image to the central Bessel lobe. We report intensity as pixel counts / exposure time, so planes recorded with different exposures are comparable. We extract the on-axis intensity $I(r=0, z)$ by fitting a $J_0^2$ function to the data (see \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_jul29}), where the amplitude $A$ represents the peak intensity of the plane. From the fitted $k_r$ wavenumber, we calculate the central lobe diameter $\varnothing$ and mean radial FWHM $\langle \mathrm{FWHM} \rangle_r$. The measured Bessel zone $Z_R$ is the interval between the outermost points at which $I(r=0, z)$ falls to $50\%$ of its maximum, and $\Delta Z_R$ is the length of that interval. The ripple metric is the peak-to-peak variation of $I(r=0, z)$ within the measured Bessel zone, normalized to the mean on-axis intensity.
The beam profiling software, analysis notebooks, and mechanical designs are open source\footnotemark[2].

\footnotetext[2]{\url{https://github.com/leigh-johnson/bessel_beam_profiler}}

None of the four configurations using 1~in.\ axicons described in
\autoref{tab:configs} met the design requirements described in
\autoref{sec:background}. Three of the five failed on measured data.
The longest Bessel zone was ${>}\SI{0.83}{\meter}$ against a
\SI{1}{\meter} target. The largest central lobe was
\SI{125.6}{\micro\meter} FWHM against the \SI{300}{\micro\meter}
needed to contain the atom cloud. The on-axis intensity varied by
48-63\% of the mean, so it was not uniform. Requirements~4
and~5 (tests concerned with the two science-chamber mirrors) are deferred to future work (\autoref{sec:future_work}).

The measured Bessel zone for each configuration is summarized in \autoref{tab:results_summary} and \autoref{fig:measured_window_envelopes_lobe_diam_colored}, along with the modeled geometric and QDHT ranges. We evaluated four candidates, described in \autoref{tab:configs}. The QDHT simulation consistently predicted the starting point of the Bessel range for three candidates within $10\%$ agreement. In two of the three modeled candidates, the Bessel zone length was within $8\%$ agreement with the QDHT simulation. The geometric model consistently predicted the start of the Bessel zone within $3.6\%$ to $5.6\%$ agreement but overestimated the Bessel zone length by $+42\%$ to $+89\%$.

Candidate \#1 was the baseline configuration, and it established how closely the models describe a well-aligned system. The measured zone length fell within \SI{1}{\centi\meter} of the QDHT prediction, the closest agreement of any candidate, and the core diameter and FWHM tracked the ideal $J_0^2$ values to within 2\% at every plane inside the Bessel zone.

Candidate \#2 used a $2\times$ magnification telescope before axicon~1 to expand the Gaussian input beam. Expanding the input beam substantially increased the Bessel zone length ($\Delta Z_R > 0.83$ m), but at the cost of worse on-axis intensity ripples.
The spacing $L_{12} = \SI{240}{\milli\meter}$ was the maximum allowed by the 1~in.\ aperture of axicon~2, so this configuration represents the longest Bessel zone reachable with 1~in.\ optics. Space limitations on the optics table prevented full characterization, so $Z_R$ and $\Delta Z_R$ are lower bounds. Core diameter and FWHM tracked the ideal values to within 5\%.

Candidate \#3 repeated candidate \#1 with the axicon spacings changed (\autoref{tab:configs}) to shrink the hollow Gaussian beam reaching axicon~3. It produced the shortest zone among the candidates. The core diameter and FWHM tracked the ideal values to within 1\%. Unfortunately, this comparison is confounded by sub-optimal alignment of axicons~2 and~3, which would contribute to a reduced $\Delta Z_R$ (\autoref{fig:annulus_composites}). We cannot separate the effect of the spacing change from the effect of the misalignment, and this candidate also showed the largest QDHT disagreement in zone length.

Candidate \#4 repeated the setup of Candidate \#3, with a $2\times$ magnification telescope placed within the Bessel zone at $z = \SI{120}{\centi\meter}$ after axicon~3. The telescope doubled the transverse core size as expected, but,
surprisingly, left the Bessel zone length nearly unchanged
($\Delta Z_R = 0.32 \pm 0.01$~m vs.\ $0.30 \pm 0.01$~m for \#3) rather
than stretching it by the expected longitudinal magnification. We
examine this result in Sec.~\ref{sec:telescope}.

Candidate \#2's Bessel zone was not fully characterized due to space limitations on the optics table, so the measured $Z_R$ and $\Delta Z_R$ should be interpreted as lower bounds. No QDHT model was developed for candidate \#4, which placed a $2\times$ magnification telescope inside the Bessel zone.}

\subsection{Ripples in On-Axis Intensity}
The ripple \% metric is defined in \autoref{sec:metrics}. The on-axis intensity ripples varied by 48-63\% of the mean within every candidate's Bessel zone (see \autoref{fig:measured_window_envelopes_lobe_diam_colored}), which ruled out all four under the uniformity requirement.

In early design/modeling stages, we were unsure if the on-axis intensity ripples were a numerical artifact of the simulation sampling/aliasing. However, the ripples persisted across a wide range of sampling parameters. Initial measurements with a manual translation stage (see figure TODO) were too coarse to resolve them, but finer scans with the motorized gantry revealed on-axis intensity modulations in agreement with those predicted analytically by Rao~\cite{raoOnaxisIntensityModulationfree2018} and reproduced by the QDHT simulations.

\subsection{Axicon Alignment Drift}
To test repeatability, we re-measured three $z$ positions of candidate
\#2's Bessel zone five days after the original scan
(\autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_jul29} vs. \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_aug3}). We reproduced the intensity ripples. The fitted core diameters agreed within a few percent, but the peak intensity dropped from 0.48 to 0.33 counts/\si{\micro\second}, suggesting the drift did not occur uniformly. In \autoref{fig:placement_cmp_report_aug3}, the X-chord (blue dots) shifted more than the Y-chord (orange dots), resulting in a slight astigmatism. Fixing it required systematically adjusting the horizontal axes of all XY translation mounts to locate the source.

\subsection{Effect of M=2x Telescope}\label{sec:telescope}
Candidate \#4 placed a $2\times$ magnification telescope inside the
Bessel zone, $z = \SI{120}{\centi\meter}$ after axicon~3
(\autoref{tab:configs}), to understand the effect of magnification on the Bessel beam. The mean core diameter of \SI{268.1}{\micro\meter} is within $7.8\%$ of
twice the ideal core size, confirming the expected $2\times$ transverse
magnification in the XY plane. This behavior was similar to the effect on a Gaussian beam, where an $M=2$ telescope doubles the waist of the Gaussian beam.

The peak intensity dropped to 0.32 counts/\si{\micro\second},
compared to 2.00 for the identical optics without the telescope
(candidate \#3). Spreading the same power over a $4\times$ larger area ($M^2 = 4$) alone would predict 0.50 counts/\si{\micro\second}. The remaining loss would only be consistent with $\approx$0.7 counts/\si{\micro\second} ($\approx$35\% of the incident power) lost at the telescope lenses, so the coating and alignment of this configuration should be re-checked.

Candidate \#4's dataset is the smallest we collected (241 images,
\autoref{tab:configs}), it has a gap near $z \approx \SI{0.2}{\meter}$,
and its $z$ axis is referenced to the telescope output lens rather than
to axicon~3. Subject to those limitations, we did not observe the
expected longitudinal magnification of the Bessel zone. The measured
length was $\Delta Z_R = 0.32 \pm 0.01$~m, against
$0.30 \pm 0.01$~m for candidate \#3 without the telescope. The missing planes near
$z \approx \SI{0.2}{\meter}$ may contain a ripple peak that would extend
the measured zone, in which case the magnification is present but
unresolved. A re-scan of this configuration is needed to confirm the zone length scales as expected when a telescope acts on an already-formed Bessel zone.

\section{Conclusion and Perspective}
We designed, built, and characterized a three-axicon Bessel beam
system and evaluated four candidates for the interrogation beam in a \SI{1}{\meter}
vertical strontium optical lattice clock. Every configuration we tested
produced a Bessel region with an identifiable core lobe in the cross-section. The measured core lobe diameters tracked
the ideal $J_0^2$ values to within 8\%. The QDHT wave-optics model predicted the start of the Bessel zone within 10\% and, where we could measure it cleanly, the zone length within 8\%.

The tested three-axicon system, built from the present 1~in.\ optics,
did not meet the clock-interrogation requirements. The longest measured
zone (${>}0.83$~m) fell short of the \SI{1}{\meter} target. The central
lobe reached at most \SI{125.6}{\micro\meter} FWHM, well under the
\SI{300}{\micro\meter} specified atom cloud diameter. The on-axis
intensity ripple of 48-63\% far exceeded any acceptable uniformity
threshold for clock interrogation.

Another open issue is day-to-day alignment drift, which needs characterization before any configuration is adopted. These results motivate the future work below,
as well as the tooling we built to reduce the cost of every iteration
that follows (automated beam profiler, QDHT simulator, axicon alignment
tooling).

\subsection{Future Work}\label{sec:future_work}
Several open questions remain. First, we have not measured how the first $1/2$~in. steering mirror (mirror 1 in \autoref{fig:theory_geometric_model}) affects the beam profile. The mirror sits upstream of the Bessel zone, and diffraction from its edges could distort the structure of the beam before it reaches the atoms. Second, axicons with 2~in. diameter have been ordered to raise the cap on $L_{12}$ that the 1~in. aperture imposes. Third, day-to-day alignment drift limited the repeatability of our measurements, so the beam alignment should be monitored continuously. The automated alignment tool we built\footnotemark[3] could be run between measurements to detect drift and alert when remediation is required. The alignment tool could also be extended to drive motorized translation mounts, ensuring the axicon lenses are always aligned to a certain tolerance. Ultimately, the final system will interrogate strontium atoms at \SI{698}{\nano\meter} rather than the \SI{650}{\nano\meter} of our test laser diode. The selected configuration will likely need to be re-characterized with the clock laser, since the transverse wavenumber $k_r$ and Bessel zone geometry scale with wavelength.
